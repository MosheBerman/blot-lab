Ink age: were the marks added later?

Every bolding, partial spill, and blackout in the coded-letters corpus was compared ink-against-ink with its own surrounding words. Verdict: the marks are the writers’ own work — same-session emphasis, pen drips, and self-redaction. Nothing indicates a later hand.

01

Method

Ink color cannot be carbon-dated from a photo. What it can do is far more useful here: tell whether a mark was made with the same pen and ink as the surrounding writing, or a different one.

For each mark, ink pixels were sampled in CIELAB and expressed relative to the local paper color from the same photo: darkness depth dL = paperL − inkL, and chromaticity da, db vs. paper. Distance dP is the Euclidean distance between two paper-relative signatures. Stroke width comes from a distance transform on the ink mask. Because every comparison is intra-photo (mark vs. neighboring words in the same image), lighting, exposure, and white balance cancel out — which matters, because the top of letter 33’s first page is overexposed to clipping while its middle reads normally.

dP(target, ref) = √( ΔdL² + Δda² + Δdb² )  ·  paper-relative, same photo only

Decision rule, stated before running: umlaut dots, i-dots, and neighboring ordinary words calibrate the same ink band; the third author’s blue marginalia (a known different hand) anchors the different ink end. Retracing = same hue + ≥1.35× darker + ≥1.4× wider strokes.

EXIF audit

Every working image was checked: 0 EXIF tags on all letter photos and crops. The Apple Notes scan-to-PDF pipeline and the crop exports stripped all exposure and white-balance metadata; the PDF renders carry only resolution and orientation tags. There is therefore no EXIF to normalize by — and none is needed, since the paper-relative intra-photo comparison is immune to per-photo lighting differences by construction. Cross-photo absolute color comparison was not attempted and would not be valid.

Honest limits

Different ink means a different pen, or the same pen in a much later session — color alone cannot separate those. Same ink rules out a different pen but not a later touch-up with the same pen. Water damage and foxing were avoided by bounding-box placement, and every annotated figure below was visually verified box-by-box (two misplacements — a page-edge shadow caught in a für box, a wrong line in an umlaut crop — were found this way and corrected before measuring).

02

Calibration: the marks above “u”

Tiny supra-letter marks are the perfect calibrators: if the method is sound, a genuine umlaut dot must match its own word — and then the same test tells diacritics apart from stray spills, and confirms readings against m/n confusion.

Umlaut dots and i-dots match their words same pen

Letter 33, p.1 — durchführen, für, die

ü-dots vs für7.07matchsame ink
i-dot vs die8.72matchsame ink
ü-dots vs durchführen25.18match (dC=3)same pen, light touch

The durchführen dots are lighter than the word body (quick pen touches take less ink) but identical in hue — the calibration behaves exactly as a same-pen mark should. Consequence for readings: a mark above a u whose signature matches the word is a diacritic, not a spill and not part of a neighboring letter. That is how für, durchführen, and die are confirmed against m/n misreadings: the dots belong to the words.

Control: the third author’s blue marginalia (reversed writing in the top margin, a known different hand) scores dP = 52–58 against Josef’s ink — the method cleanly separates different inks. Ordinary neighboring words vary at dP = 1–25, driven by pen wetness, not hue.

Words that look darker than their line: retracing (going over the word twice) or a fresh pen dip?

Hue is identical to the line (chroma distance ≈3); the word is 1.4–1.5× darker with 1.43× wider strokes. That is the double-application signature: the writer went over cheder a second time with the same pen. Emphasis — deliberate, by the author. Whether it happened mid-letter or on a later re-reading with the same pen is not datable from ink alone, and the ink does not choose between those.

A heavy same-pen re-dip deposits more blue dye and would look exactly like this, so same-pen emphasis remains plausible; but unlike cheder, the hue shift means a different (bluer) pen cannot be excluded. The one mark in the survey where added later with another pen survives as a live alternative.

06

What this means for “added after the fact”

Fourteen of fifteen marks resolve to the writer’s own pen: emphasis retracing (cheder), fresh dips (Du), same-session insertions (Dankend, Ausgelöst), pen drips and smears (the Jo blot, the smudge, the speck), and self-redaction (both blackouts). The fifteenth, the aerogramme dab, is a spill of a different blue — an accident of handling, not a later hand’s message. The known-different control (the third author’s marginalia) separates cleanly, so the method is not simply calling everything “same.”

The one genuine ambiguity is letter 42’s Der: heavier and bluer than its line. Same-pen heavy emphasis and a different pen remain both live. Everything else the ink can say points one way: the marked-up words were marked up by the people who wrote the letters, and nothing in the ink suggests anyone came back later to add, hide, or emphasize.
