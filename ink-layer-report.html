Postcard Ink-Layer Physics

Can text blacked out on 1940s postcards be recovered from RGB photographs? Card stock, ink penetration, saturation, Kubelka-Munk hiding limits, and the published algorithms. Research date: 2026-10-13.

Case A: heavy black-ink multi-pass scribble block (~1.5 x 6 cm) obliterating ~2 lines of handwriting, applied with the writer's own pen. Case B: blue fountain-pen ink dabs placed directly over words, in the same blue ink as the surrounding handwriting. Available data: only RGB photographs (~150-300 dpi equivalent). No multispectral or infrared capture.

1. Postcard stock: German Reichspost postcards, ca. 1940

(a/b) The 1924 World Postal Congress set the postcard maximum at 10.5 x 15 cm; the DIN A6 "Normalformat" was introduced in Germany on 1 October 1927, so the 1940-41 cards are almost certainly A6 stock (b). International rules admit postcard grammage of 150 to 500 g/m2 (b; UPU rules). Modern specs corroborate the low end: PostNL requires min 170 g/m2 (b), Royal Mail Wholesale min 200 gsm (b). Typical modern postcard paper is quoted at 250-350 gsm (b, weak source). US rules require postcards 0.18-0.41 mm thick (b).

(c) Inference: German Reichspost Ganzsachen of 1940 were machine-made wood-pulp board, almost surely 200-300 g/m2, i.e. ~0.25-0.35 mm thick at typical board density. No published official Reichspost 1940 grammage spec was found; this is bracketed by the standards above. Wartime stock likely had higher groundwood/recycled content and less sizing (c, unverified for Reichspost specifically).

(b) Comparison: onionskin/airmail paper is 25-39 g/m2 (b), a measured 35 gsm sample 46 um thick (b). Postcard stock is roughly 6-10x the grammage and 5-7x the thickness of onionskin. Consequence: far more fibre mass to absorb ink, and far more scattering mass to hide what is inside.

Composition (a/b/c): uncoated sized board - cellulose fibres, mineral filler, rosin/gelatin size; fibres are hydrophilic cellulose ~10 um diameter (b).

2. Ink penetration depth

(b) Fibre-scale STXM mapping of iron-gall ink (Gimat et al., 2015): paper fibres ~10 um diameter; ink does not penetrate uniformly - gallic acid stays mostly in the outer cell wall, Fe(III) gallate precipitates during migration, only small amounts of iron reach the fibre interior. Gum arabic binder "significantly jeopardizes" penetration; the ink stays at the fibre surface.

(b) Printed ink film: X-ray microtomography + profilometry (Myllys et al., 2015) measured a typical ink layer ~2.5 um deep, about 1/40 of a ~100 um sheet, with large spatial variation.

(a) Transport law: Lucas-Washburn, penetration depth proportional to sqrt(time) x sqrt(pore radius). Water-based inks deviate via fibre swelling and capillary saturation (b).

(c) Inference for fountain-pen dye inks on postcard board: a single stroke deposits a film a few um wet; dye + water wicks to order tens of microns - a few fibre diameters, not hundreds. Fountain-pen writing rarely shows through 250-300 g/m2 board, bounding single-pass penetration well below the ~300 um sheet: very roughly 10-50 um. A heavy multi-pass scribble does not drill through the full 0.3 mm: penetration is rate-limited (sqrt(t)) and the board is sized. Instead it saturates the top fibre layers and pools at the surface - which is why a scribble looks black and glossy. The under-writing's dye already occupies fibre sites in the same top tens of um, so the second application largely re-dissolves and mixes with dye in the already-wetted zone rather than stacking as a clean separate stratum. The two layers are intermixed at fibre scale, not a neat sandwich.

3. Absorption saturation

(a) Liquid uptake follows the Bristow/Lucas-Washburn form: V(t) = K_r + K_a*sqrt(t - t_w), with V in ml/m2 (a/b; Selim et al., 1997). On plain paper, penetration rate is at least 20x the evaporation rate - drying is penetration-dominated (b).

(b) Pigment particles larger than the pore size are filtered and stay at the surface (b) - directly relevant: carbon-black pigment in black pen ink stays surficial while its vehicle penetrates.

(b) A hard capacity number: Royal Mail's paper spec requires water absorbency of 15-35 g of water per m2 in 1 minute (Cobb-type test). That is a genuine saturation capacity.

Max uptake in one minute: 0.023 ml

A few pen passes deliver of order this volume; beyond it, ink cannot enter and must spread laterally or sit on the surface. Saturation is reached quickly and shallowly: there is no "deeper reservoir" that would preserve the under-layer as a separable signal.

4. Kubelka-Munk layered reflectance and the information-theoretic limit

(a) Kubelka-Munk (1931): two diffuse fluxes through plane-parallel layers; each material needs only absorption K and back-scattering S. The remission function F(R) = (1-R)^2/(2R) = K/S linearises reflectance. The theory was built to answer "the thickness of paint needed to obscure the substrate" - exactly our question in reverse. For mixtures, K and S add linearly (Duncan two-constant mixing); K/S space is where layered inks combine linearly (b; Zhang et al., arXiv:2104.04884). Ideal glaze: R = R_g * exp(-2*a_0*X).

(a/c) The under-ink contributes to measured reflectance iff the top layer is not optically opaque: light must make the round trip (down through top ink, scatter, back up) with non-negligible intensity. That requires K_top * X_top <~ 1 in the camera's bands. As K_top*X_top grows, the under-layer's contribution decays exponentially toward zero.

(a) The information-theoretic limit. When the top ink reaches complete hiding - reflectance R becomes R_infinity of the top ink alone, independent of the substrate - the reflected light carries zero mutual information about what lies beneath. No algorithm on reflected light (RGB or hyperspectral) can recover the under-text then; the signal is absent, not merely weak. This is a hard physical limit, not a signal-processing challenge.

Caveats (a/b): K-M assumes diffuse fluxes and homogeneous layers - handwriting violates homogeneity, but the hiding-power conclusion is robust. K-M fails in strongly absorbing materials - precisely the saturated-black regime, where the failure mode is more opacity, not less. And even where some under-signal survives, 3 RGB channels must separate 4+ unknowns per pixel - the inverse problem is severely underdetermined from RGB alone (c).

Signal decays as e-2KX. K·X = 1 is the boundary of the thin regime; K·X ≥ 2–3 is complete hiding. A saturated black scribble sits at K·X ≫ 1.

5. Existing algorithms

5a. Multispectral / hyperspectral imaging

  • Archimedes Palimpsest (b): 6 bands (tungsten + UV 365 nm), then 11 LED bands 365-870 nm + broadband + raking light at 25 px/mm; PCA/classification on the cube; synchrotron XRF mapped iron in the undertext; PCA on RGB of UV fluorescence recovered the Hypereides text. Input: multispectral rig and/or XRF - not RGB-only. Success needs different chemistry/spectra between layers; large areas still failed.
  • Marie Antoinette retouched correspondence (Michelin et al. 2021, b): macro-XRF + PCA separated superimposed iron-gall inks because the two inks had distinct elemental compositions. Fails for same-ink.
  • Faded handwriting (Wiley, 2026, b): hyperspectral + minimum-noise-fraction rotation restores faded (not over-inked) writing.
  • Verdict for us: every demonstrated success exploits spectral or elemental contrast between the layers. Case B (same blue dye) and Case A (saturated black over blue) offer no such contrast in the visible, and we have no multispectral capture anyway.

5b. Blind source separation / ICA on RGB scans

  • Tonazzini et al. (b): ICA on RGB scans separating recto/verso/background as 3 sources. But Wolf demonstrates the linear mixing assumption is unjustified for ink on paper (b).
  • Merrikh-Bayat et al. (b): nonlinear (linear-quadratic) BSS for show-through. Results limited by scanner blur.
  • Survey (b): ICA, dual-layer MRF, CRF, colour-decorrelation, PCA+segmentation, EM for bleed-through/show-through. Documented success: faint bleed-through on thin paper. Documented failure: when the interference is as dark as the writing, "practically impossible to remove show-through" (b). None of this literature addresses opaque over-inking - the models assume the under-signal is a weak additive ghost, the opposite of a saturated scribble.
  • Tangentially: RGB statistics can distinguish ink types - fractal + RGB analysis separated five different black iron-gall inks (b; Nature Sci. Rep. 2023). Useful for which ink, not for under what.

5c. Kubelka-Munk-based spectral unmixing

  • Zhang et al., arXiv:2104.04884 (b): unmixing in (K/S) space, where pigment mixtures combine linearly; demonstrated for pigment identification on cultural-heritage artefacts from hyperspectral data. Not demonstrated for handwriting under over-inking, and not applicable to 3-channel RGB (underdetermined; endmembers for "same blue ink, single vs. double pass" nearly collinear).
  • K-M is, however, the right forward model: the mixture law is linear in K/S, not in RGB - any unmixing attempt should transform R to F(R)=(1-R)^2/2R first (a/c).

5d. Correction fluid / heavy redaction / same-ink overwriting

  • Same ink over same ink: forensic QD doctrine - "If it is done with the same ink as was used to write the original material, recovery will be difficult if not impossible" (b). IR recovery works only when the two inks differ chemically (b).
  • Obliterations generally: a Video Spectral Comparator with UV/IR "can reveal writing added with a different ink... exploiting variations in the way different inks respond to different wavelengths" (b).
  • Physical routes: oblique/transmitted light and microscopy for traces beneath overwritten strokes; embossing on the verso from heavy pen pressure (b); ESDA recovers indented writing from up to 7 sheets below, on documents up to 60 years old (b).
  • Digital redaction: solid black bars are the recommended redaction precisely because pixelation/blur are reversible and black bars are not (b). The only "recovery" under solid bars used font metrics + language models - irrelevant to handwriting.
  • Gap: no published method demonstrates recovery of handwriting under a heavy multi-pass pen scribble or under same-ink dabbing from RGB-only images.

6. Practical verdict (RGB photograph only)

Case A - heavy black multi-pass scribble over handwriting: hopeless from RGB reflectance. A saturated black pen layer has K*X ≫ 1 across the visible; in K-M terms it has reached complete hiding power - measured reflectance is R_infinity of the black ink, independent of the substrate. The under-signal is physically absent from reflected light, not merely hidden. The top ink's vehicle saturates the same top tens of um the under-writing occupies; the layers are fibre-scale intermixed, so no clean stratum boundary exists for any algorithm to exploit. Forensic literature offers no RGB (or any reflected-light) recovery path when the covering is opaque. The only physically plausible residual channel is 3D: pen-pressure embossing of the original strokes (oblique light/RTI, or lab ESDA - documented on 60-year-old documents), which needs re-imaging or lab access. Transmitted light fails too: a saturated black block is opaque in transmission.
Case B - blue dye dabs over blue dye handwriting (same ink): effectively hopeless for running text from the existing scans. Same chemistry means no spectral/elemental contrast for any multispectral, XRF, or IR method; forensic doctrine calls this "difficult if not impossible". What could survive: (i) at dab edges and thin spots the top layer may be sub-opaque, so the under-stroke modulates reflectance; (ii) dye-concentration gradients - the under-stroke adds dye where it lies; (iii) wicking/coffee-ring edge effects tracing the original stroke boundary; (iv) fibre disturbance/embossing. From 150-300 dpi scans these are second-order effects at the noise floor (~85-170 um/px vs ~300-500 um strokes). Any "recovery" would be fragmentary stroke fragments at dab edges, not running text.

What is actually worth trying (ranked)

  1. Re-photograph (still RGB sensor): transmitted-light + oblique-light imaging. Backlighting turns the problem into optical-density measurement: dye concentration differences show as density differences even with identical chemistry. Oblique light captures embossed topography. Highest expected value, especially for Case B. (c)
  2. K/S-transform + constrained unmixing (or ICA/PCA) on high-resolution RGB, Case B only. Convert R to F(R)=(1-R)^2/2R per channel (the space where K-M says inks mix linearly), sample local endmembers (bare paper / single-pass blue / dab blue), solve the constrained mixture per pixel. Expect at best stroke fragments at dab edges. Needs a fresh 600-1200 dpi 16-bit capture. (c)
  3. 3D/topographic capture for stroke embossing (Case A and B). Photometric stereo / RTI from an RGB camera, or lab ESDA for indented writing (b). Bypasses ink optics entirely and reads the pressure record. For Case A it is the only non-destructive avenue with nonzero probability. (c)

Could not verify

  • Official Reichspost 1940 postcard grammage/thickness spec: not found; bracketed at ~200-300 g/m2 / ~0.25-0.35 mm from UPU-era postal standards.
  • Wartime (1940-41) paper composition of Reichspost Ganzsachen: not verified - affects exact penetration numbers but not the verdict.
  • Quantitative fountain-pen dye penetration depth on sized board: no direct published measurement found; bounded at ~10-50 um (marked (c)).
  • No published RGB-only recovery of text under heavy pen scribble or same-ink over-dabbing was found - a literature-search negative, not a proof of impossibility.
