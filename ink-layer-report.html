Blot lab · research briefing

How thick is the card, how deep does the ink go — and can the layers be split?

The physics answer, from the published literature on 1940s postcard stock, ink penetration, and layered reflectance: the card is about 300 µm thick, the ink lives in the top few tens of microns, and both inks end up mixed in the same saturated zone. That geometry — not a missing algorithm — is what decides whether anything under a blot can be recovered from a photograph.

Case A

Heavy black multi-pass scribble

Hopeless from reflected light.

A saturated black layer reaches complete hiding in Kubelka–Munk terms: the reflected light is the top ink's own reflectance, independent of what lies beneath. The under-signal is physically absent from an RGB photo — not buried, gone. a b

Case B

Blue dabs over blue handwriting, same ink

Effectively hopeless for running text.

Same ink means no spectral or elemental contrast for any method to exploit. Only second-order traces survive — extra dye density and embossing — and at 150–300 dpi they sit at the noise floor. Thin dab edges might yield stroke fragments from a high-res re-scan, nothing more. b c

The cards are almost certainly DIN A6 "Normalformat" stock: the format was introduced in Germany on 1 October 1927 and had established itself through the 1930s–40s, under the 10.5 × 15 cm international maximum set by the 1924 World Postal Congress. b Modern postal specs corroborate the low end of the bracket — PostNL requires a minimum of 170–200 g/m² for cards, Royal Mail Wholesale a minimum of 200 gsm — and US rules put postcard thickness at 0.18–0.41 mm. b

The board was uncoated and sized: cellulose fibres with mineral filler and rosin/gelatin size to make it writable. Early postcard board was yellowish-brown so soiling would show less. a b Wartime stock likely carried more groundwood and recycled content and less sizing than peacetime board — plausible, but not verified for Reichspost card stock specifically. c

Against onionskin: airmail/onionskin paper runs 25–39 g/m² (tariff definitions put airmail paper under 40 g/m²). The postcard is roughly 6–10× the grammage and 5–7× the thickness — about 0.3 mm against about 0.05 mm. b c

That cuts both ways for ink work. The card has far more fibre mass per unit area to absorb ink — and far more scattering mass to hide whatever is inside it. Thin airmail stock is the forgiving case for see-through recovery; a 300 µm board is not.

How deep does the ink go?

Depth of a typical printed ink film — about 1/40 of its sheet b
10–50 µm
Bounded estimate for one fountain-pen stroke's penetration into sized board c
√t
Penetration grows with the square root of time (Lucas–Washburn) — extra passes add little depth a

At fibre scale, synchrotron X-ray microscopy (STXM) of iron-gall ink in paper shows penetration is not uniform: gallic acid stays mostly in the outer cell wall of the ~10 µm fibres, the iron-gallate pigment precipitates as it migrates, and only small amounts of iron reach the fibre's interior. Add gum arabic binder and the ink grows viscous and "significantly jeopardizes its ability to penetrate the fibre — the ink thus remains at the surface of the fibre." b

Transport follows Lucas–Washburn: depth grows as √(time) × √(mean pore radius). Water-based inks deviate through fibre swelling and capillary saturation, but the square-root law is why dwell time buys so little extra depth. a b

For fountain-pen dye ink on this board, a single stroke lays down a film a few microns wet; dye and water wick into the sized board to a depth of order tens of microns — a few fibre diameters, not hundreds. Fountain-pen writing rarely shows through 250–300 g/m² board, which bounds single-pass penetration well under the ~300 µm sheet thickness: very roughly 10–50 µm. No direct published measurement of dye depth on sized board was found; this is a bound, not a measurement. c

The scribble does not drill

A heavy multi-pass scribble deposits far more liquid, but penetration is rate-limited (√t) and the board is sized — it does not punch through the full 0.3 mm. Instead it saturates the top fibre layers and pools at the surface, which is exactly why a scribble looks black and glossy. The handwriting's dye already occupies fibre sites in the same top tens of microns, so the second ink largely re-dissolves and mixes with dye in the already-wetted zone. The two inks are intermixed at fibre scale — not stacked as a clean two-layer sandwich any algorithm could peel apart. c

04

Saturation: how much ink can a square centimetre hold?

Paper takes up liquid by the Bristow / Lucas–Washburn law — a fast surface-roughness term, then a square-root-of-time absorption term:

V(t) = Kr + Ka · √(t − tw) V = absorbed volume per unit area (ml/m²) · Kr = surface-roughness uptake (ml/m²) · Ka = absorption coefficient (ml/(m²·ms0.5)), measured with a Bristow tester. a b

Measured on plain paper with water-based inks, the penetration rate is at least 20× the evaporation rate — drying is penetration-dominated. Uptake can run faster than the ideal law (fibre swelling, capillary expansion) or slower (capillary saturation), and surface chemistry matters. b

One asymmetry matters for Case A: pigment particles larger than the paper's pore size are filtered out and stay at the surface while the liquid vehicle penetrates. Carbon-black pigment in a black pen ink is exactly such a particle — the black stays surficial and optically dense while its carrier soaks in. b

A hard capacity number: postal paper specs require water absorbency of 15–35 g of water per m² in one minute (Cobb-type test, BS EN 20535). A square metre of card drinks only 15–35 ml in a full minute. Scale that to the scribble block and saturation arrives after a few pen passes; beyond it, ink cannot enter and must spread sideways or sit on the surface. b

So saturation is reached quickly and shallowly: the first pass wets the top fibre layers; later passes only push surface dye concentration toward its optical maximum — the dense black. There is no deeper reservoir that keeps the under-layer preserved as a separate signal. c

05

Kubelka–Munk: when does the under-ink still show?

Kubelka–Munk theory (1931) models two diffuse light fluxes through layered material with just two constants per material: absorption K and back-scattering S. It was built to answer literally this question — its "hiding power" is the paint thickness needed to obscure a substrate. a

R∞ = 1 + K/S − √((K/S)² + 2(K/S))   ·   F(R∞) = (1 − R∞)² / (2R∞) = K/S Reflectance of an opaque layer, and the remission function that linearises it. For mixtures, K and S add by concentration — so layered inks combine linearly in K/S space, not in RGB. Any unmixing attempt should transform R → F(R) first. a b

For an ideal glaze — a pure absorber that scatters little, which a dye ink approximates — the substrate's contribution survives attenuated exponentially with top-layer thickness:

R = Rg · exp(−2a0X) Rg = substrate reflectance · a0 = top-layer absorption · X = top-layer thickness.

The under-ink still contributes to what a camera measures only while Ktop·Xtop ≲ 1 in the camera's bands — a thin or weakly absorbing top layer. Past that, the under-layer's contribution decays exponentially toward zero. a c

The information-theoretic limit

When the top ink reaches complete hiding — measured reflectance equal to R∞ of the top ink alone, independent of the substrate — the reflected light carries zero mutual information about what lies beneath. No algorithm on reflected light, RGB or hyperspectral, can recover the under-text. The signal is not weak; it is absent. That is a hard physical limit, not a signal-processing challenge. a

Two caveats, both unfavourable: K–M assumes homogeneous layers (handwriting violates this, but the hiding conclusion is robust), and K–M fails in strongly absorbing materials — precisely the saturated-black regime — where the failure mode is more opacity, not less. And even where some under-signal survives, 3 RGB channels must separate at least 4 unknowns per pixel (two inks × concentrations, paper, noise): severely underdetermined. b c

06

The algorithm survey: what exists, what it needs

Every published success at reading under ink exploits spectral or elemental contrast between the layers, or non-optical information. None demonstrates recovery of handwriting under a heavy pen scribble, or under same-ink dabbing, from RGB alone — that gap is a literature-search negative, not a proof of impossibility. b

Multispectral / hyperspectral imaging

Needs: multispectral rig — not RGB

Archimedes Palimpsest: pages imaged in 6 bands (tungsten + UV 365 nm), then 11 LED bands from 365–870 nm plus raking light at 25 px/mm; PCA/classification on the registered cube; synchrotron X-ray fluorescence mapped iron in the undertext ink. b

Marie Antoinette's retouched letters: macro-XRF + PCA separated superimposed iron-gall inks — because the two inks had distinct elemental compositions. Faded (not over-inked) handwriting has also been restored with hyperspectral imaging. b

For our cardsSuccess requires different chemistry/spectra between layers and capture gear we don't have. Case B is the same ink; Case A is saturated in every visible band. Large palimpsest areas stayed unreadable even with the full rig.

Blind source separation / ICA on RGB

Needs: RGB scan (sometimes + verso)

What it does: Tonazzini et al. applied ICA to RGB scans to separate recto / verso / background as three sources; later work uses nonlinear (linear-quadratic) BSS modelling show-through, pigment penetration and vehicle-oil components, plus MRF/CRF and colour-decorrelation variants. b

Documented limits: Wolf showed the linear mixing assumption is unjustified for ink on paper — pixel colour doesn't combine linearly from sources. The literature targets faint bleed-through on thin paper; when the interference is as dark as the writing, removal is "practically impossible" with these models. Scanner blur further limits the nonlinear method. b

For our cardsThe whole bleed-through literature assumes the under-signal is a weak additive ghost — the opposite of a saturated scribble. None of it addresses opaque over-inking.

Kubelka–Munk spectral unmixing

Needs: hyperspectral reflectance + endmembers

What it does: unmixing in (K/S) space, where pigment mixtures combine linearly (Zhang et al., 2021), demonstrated for pigment identification on cultural-heritage objects from hyperspectral data, assuming diffuse illumination, high optical thickness and known endmember spectra. b

As a forward model K–M is still the right frame for RGB: it says the mixture law is linear in K/S, so any attempt should work on F(R) = (1−R)²/2R, not raw RGB. a c

For our cardsNot demonstrated for handwriting under over-inking, and not applicable to 3-channel RGB: the system is underdetermined, and the endmembers for "same blue ink, single vs. double pass" are nearly collinear.

Forensic questioned-document practice

Needs: IR / VSC / lab examination

Spectral route: a Video Spectral Comparator with UV/IR reveals writing covered by a different ink, exploiting differing wavelength responses. IR photography works only when the two inks differ chemically — IR passes through the upper layer and is absorbed below. b

Physical routes: oblique/transmitted light and microscopy for traces beneath the stroke; embossing on the verso from heavy pen pressure; ESDA recovers indented writing from up to 7 sheets below, on documents up to 60 years old. b

"If it is done with the same ink as was used to write the original material, recovery will be difficult if not impossible." — forensic questioned-document doctrine, on obliteration with the same ink b

For our cardsCase B is precisely the doctrine's impossible case. Case A's remaining forensic channel is not optical at all — it's the pressure record (see ranked steps).

Digital redaction research

Needs: RGB

Solid black bars are the recommended redaction precisely because pixelation and blur are reversible and black bars are not. The only published "recovery" under solid bars (Lopresti & Spitz, 2005) used font metrics plus language models — a print-text trick with no bearing on handwriting. b

Separately, RGB statistics can distinguish ink types — fractal + RGB analysis separated five different black iron-gall inks on scans. Useful for "which ink," not for "what's under it." b

For our cardsConfirms the shape of the problem from the redactor's side: an opaque block is the one redaction that works.

07

Case B: the traces that could survive

Same ink kills every spectral lever, but four second-order traces could in principle remain. All are inference, and all sit near the noise floor of the existing scans: at 150–300 dpi a pixel spans ~85–170 µm while a pen stroke is ~300–500 µm wide — marginal sampling, with JPEG/scan noise dominating the tiny concentration differences. c

Sub-opaque edges
Where a dab thins at its edges (K·X ≲ 1), the under-stroke can still modulate reflectance — recoverable in principle only where the dab didn't fully cover.
Dye-density sum
The under-stroke adds dye where it lies, so total dye density is slightly higher along the hidden stroke than in the dab alone.
Wicking edges
Chromatography / coffee-ring dye separation at the stroke boundary may trace the original stroke's outline.
Embossing
Fibre disturbance and pen-pressure indentation from the original writing — a topographic, not optical, record.

Realistic yield, even if pursued: fragmentary stroke fragments at dab edges, not running text — and only from a fresh high-resolution capture. Transmitted light will not rescue Case A either: a saturated black block is opaque in transmission too. c

08

What is actually worth trying, ranked

  1. Re-photograph with transmitted + oblique light

    Still an ordinary RGB camera — but backlighting turns the problem from reflectance into optical-density measurement: dye-concentration differences (hidden stroke + dab vs. dab alone) show as density differences even with identical ink chemistry. Oblique light captures embossed stroke topography at the same time. The single highest-expected-value step, especially for Case B. c, from Beer–Lambert/transmission physics a and forensic transmitted/oblique examination practice b.

  2. K/S-transform + constrained unmixing on high-res RGB — Case B only

    Convert R → F(R) = (1−R)²/2R per channel (the space where K–M says inks mix linearly), sample local endmembers — bare paper, single-pass blue, dab blue — and solve the constrained linear mixture per pixel; or run ICA/PCA on the RGB cube to isolate the maximum-variance "extra dye" component. Expect at best stroke fragments at dab edges. Needs a fresh ≥600–1200 dpi, 16-bit capture; the existing scans are too coarse. No published success exists for this configuration. c

  3. Topographic capture of the embossing — Cases A and B

    Photometric stereo / RTI from an RGB camera, or lab ESDA for indented writing (documented through up to 7 sheets and 60-year-old paper b). This bypasses ink optics entirely and reads the pressure record instead. For Case A it is the only non-destructive avenue with nonzero probability. c

09

Open questions — what the research could not verify

  • Official Reichspost 1940 grammage/thickness spec: not found. The 200–300 g/m² / 0.25–0.35 mm figures are bracketed from UPU-era postal standards and marked as inference throughout.
  • Wartime paper composition: the groundwood content and sizing level of 1940–41 Reichspost Ganzsachen stock is unverified. It would move the exact penetration numbers, not the verdict.
  • Quantitative dye-penetration depth on sized board: no direct published measurement in microns was found. The 10–50 µm figure is bounded by fibre-scale STXM data, bleed-through behaviour, and board thickness.
  • The "no published method" claim for RGB-only recovery under heavy scribble or same-ink dabbing is a literature-search negative — an absence found by searching, not a proof of impossibility.
